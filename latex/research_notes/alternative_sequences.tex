\section{Improving the explanation sequence}

\subsection{Alternative explanations}

\begin{problem}\label{problem-statement:alternative-explanations}
	At every explanation step, there potentially exist numerous alternative explanation steps of equal cost: 
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Explanation steps where different combinations of constraints can be used to explain the same literal;
		\item Explanation steps of same cost that explain different literals.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\subsection{Temporality of the explanations} 
\begin{problem}\label{problem-statement:locality-explanations}
	One strategy to solve the Sudoku is to look at a specific number, for instance, the one that appear the most in the grid and try to fill in that same number in other blocks of the grid.
\end{problem}


\subsection{Optimizing the sequence}
\begin{problem}\label{problem-statement:sequence-optimisation}
	The sequence generation is greedily optimized.	At every explanation step of sequence, the cost-optimal explanation step is computed. However, there is \emph{no guarantee of optimality at the sequence level} with respect to a global metric.
\end{problem}

\begin{itemize}
	\item[Q1] What are the global metrics for quantifying the quality of an explanation sequence, i.e. optimizing the explanation sequence such that
	\begin{description}
		\item[Maximum] the cost of the most difficult explanation is as low as possible;
		\item[Average]  the average cost of the sequence is as low as possible.
	\end{description}	
\end{itemize}	


\subsection*{Naive and greedy approaches to improve explanation sequence}

\begin{description}
	\item[Least-cost] Select least cost explanation step at every step of the sequence.
	\item[Breadth-first search] Explore the whole explanation search tree O(n!) to find the least cost explanation sequence, where the cost of the explanation sequence can be used as a bound .
\end{description}

\section{Modelling Explanation sequence generation as a Travelling Salesman Problem}
The generation of an explanation sequence can be modelled as a Travelling Salesman problem (TSP) where: 
\begin{itemize}
	\item the \emph{start} is the initial interpretation of the problem;
	\item the \emph{depot} is the maximal consequence we want to explain; and
	\item and the \emph{stops} are the literals to derive.
\end{itemize}

The classical TSP is modelled with a distance matrix that quantifies the distance between the stops. 
In the case of explanation generation we are interested in, the distance between two stops is computed using the cost of explaining a literal from a given interpretation (previous stops visited). 
For explanation sequence generation,the distance matrix is not given in advance. 
However, using a call to an OCUS-oracle provides an explanation of the literal given an interpretation and its corresponding cost.

\begin{observation}(\textbf{MIP-Incrementality})
	The sets-to-hit maintained by the MIP remain valid for the full UNSAT formula used by OCUS. 
	Furthermore, in the case of the MIP-based incremental hitting set solver, the ``activation'' and ``deactivation'' of literals already explained only requires a small change to the objective of the MIP solver.
%	Keeping track of the sets-to-hit will therefore provide a better starting hitting set and consequentially the number of iterations required to find an OCUS. 
\end{observation}


\subsection{Zone-preferences in TSP}

In TSP of \cite{ROCS-and-co}, the stops are grouped into zones, where a driver prefers to first service the stations from the same zone, before transitioning to another zone.
Locality of the explanations can be mapped to the concept of zones in the TSP.
\begin{example}(Sudoku)
	 Intuitively, when solving the Sudoku, when deriving a 6 in one place, one looks at deriving the same digit in other parts of the Sudoku. Even though, there might exist an explanation of similar cost for another digit, one will prefer an explanation of the same digit in another block.
\end{example}
